There is a particular hum that surrounds the industry at the moment — but I've decided here and now I shall focus only on what's worth the time to write and what's worth the time to read. As such, and with no particular drama, I hereby draw a quiet decision of my own: I would much rather remain a software engineer, than giving in to the vortex of the hype, rushing to learn the latest lingo for the sake of the spotlight, speaking hastily about tech as if tech has indeed become the new politics, and so on. I will continue to specialise in AI engineering, but I will do so for the craft, not the spotlight. It is the craft what unites us, and it is the craft what shall prove, lest I am mistaken, as I may always be, that the human mind still has much to offer.

## The hype is not the work

The hype around AI engineering and AI engineering itself are, I am sorry to report, not the same animal. The first is a market signal — a hiring shorthand, a conference badge, a flattering noun to deploy at dinner. The second is the slower and less photogenic business of building systems that do what they claim to do, under the sort of conditions a demo would politely decline to mention. The two share a vocabulary and are therefore frequently confused, rather like identical twins of whom only one has done the reading. The hype rewards visibility. The work rewards correctness, which is a far less sociable virtue.

I do not mean to be dour about the moment. The capabilities are genuine, the leverage is genuine, and the shift in what a single attentive person can now build is genuinely remarkable. But the practice of engineering — the part I happen to love — has not been transfigured in the way the discourse insists. If anything, it has been quietly underlined. More leverage carries more consequence per decision. More automation places more weight upon the judgement that elects what to automate. The fundamentals have not become quaint; they have become load-bearing.

## What the spotlight costs

Following the spotlight has a price tag that is rarely itemised. It tugs one steadily toward whatever is loudest, which is rarely whatever is most interesting, and it quietly hands one's sense of progress over to the opinions of strangers — a transaction with very poor terms. One ends up keeping score in a currency one did not choose: titles, stages, the order in which one adopted a framework that will be unfashionable by Tuesday. It is, I suspect, an exhausting sort of race, conducted against a clock someone else is winning irrespective of one's pace.

I have nothing against ambition; I have a soft spot for it, in fact. What I am wary of is ambition that has outsourced its compass to the market — for the market, bless it, has no idea what I find meaningful. It cannot tell me which problems I should still want to be holding in my head five years from now, nor whether a given week was well spent. Only the work can do that, and only if one has stayed close enough to it to hear what it is patiently trying to say.

## Why I stay an engineer

I remain an engineer because the search for excellence in this craft is, in the most agreeable way, inexhaustible. There is always a cleaner abstraction to be found, a more honest test to be written, a system whose components know slightly less about one another than they did yesterday — which is, as far as I can tell, the closest thing the discipline has to a moral arc. There is always some problem whose true shape is concealed beneath three considerate layers of incidental complexity, awaiting an unhurried reader. That kind of patience is its own reward, and it has the great virtue of not arriving on a quarterly cadence.

Specialising in AI engineering, within that frame, is not a rebrand at all — it is a problem domain, and a delightfully demanding one. So much of the engineering discipline we have spent decades sharpening must now be turned upon systems that are non-deterministic, opaque, and unhelpfully expensive to evaluate. The harnesses, the evaluations, the feedback loops, the careful boundary between what the model is permitted to decide and what the system is obliged to enforce — these are engineering problems through and through, and they reward the same unglamorous habits that good engineering has always rewarded. The novelty is mostly in the costume.

## The meaning is in the craft

The meaning I find in this work has never arrived through being seen doing it. It arrives in smaller, less photogenic moments: when something one built behaves correctly under conditions one had not anticipated; when a system turns out to be easier to change than one had any reasonable right to expect; when a stubborn bug yields, at last, to a careful reading of the code rather than a clever guess. None of these are visible from the outside. None of them register on the hype cycle. All of them are, more or less, why I do this.

So I will carry on building, and I will carry on specialising in AI precisely where the problems are hardest and the demos are most flattering, and I will let the discourse drift past without attempting to ride it. The spotlight, in its kindly way, moves on. The craft, mercifully, does not.
